# Elicitation Moves

Probes that surface what the user has not said. The handshake verifies
transmitted intent; these moves fire on their signals to elicit the intent
that cannot arrive as a requirement — wants the user recognises only on
contact with something concrete. They are moves, not a gauntlet: a routine
commission may need none of them.

## Journey Walking

Signal: the What is being established, and every journey so far was authored
by the model and confirmed by the user.

Have the user narrate a real occasion of using the finished thing — "it's
Tuesday, you open it: what happens first?" — and keep them inside the episode
(what do you see, what do you do next) rather than letting it lift back into
requirements language. A model-authored journey tests the model's picture; a
user-narrated one surfaces wants the user could not have produced as
requirements. Use both, and weight the user's tellings.

## Concrete-Instance Forcing

Signal: intent arrives as an abstraction — "manage my references", "track my
projects".

Ask for the last real instance of the problem: when did it last bite, what
exactly happened, what did it cost? Real episodes carry constraints and
frequencies the abstraction hides — and occasionally dissolve the feature,
when the last instance turns out to be years old.

## The Disappointment Probe

Signal: capture is converging and the What reads plausible.

"It's built exactly as this file says, and you're disappointed. What went
wrong?" People recognise failure far more sharply than they specify success.
This is the complement of the surprise test: the surprise test guards the
places a competent builder's *defaults* would go wrong; the disappointment
probe surfaces the user's *unstated wants* — the things nobody wrote down
because they felt too obvious to say.

## The Success Probe

Signal: a quality is vague — "good search", "feels fast" — or the Why lacks a
felt outcome.

"Three months in — how do you know it's working?" The answer is usually the
verifiable claim the writing rules demand, in raw form: turn it into a
journey ("the user does X and sees Y") rather than recording metric-flavoured
phrasing verbatim.

## The Why-Ladder

Signal: the stated want smells like a means — a dashboard, a report, an
export — or your own surprise fires: "I would not have reached for that."

The problem framing can be an anchor exactly as a tool choice can, and the
divergent survey cannot catch this one: the survey de-anchors the *route* to
a stated outcome, while a mis-framed outcome anchors everything downstream of
it. Ask one or two rungs of "what does that buy you?" up from the stated
want. Stop the moment answers turn from project-context to life-context —
that boundary is the tell that the ladder has cleared the project's real
problem, and past it the probe becomes an interrogation. What the ladder
surfaces feeds the Why; a want that survives it unchanged is simply
confirmed.
